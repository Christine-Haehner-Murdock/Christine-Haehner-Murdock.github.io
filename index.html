Independent AI research and collaboration

Nobody really knows what AI is, not even AI. That’s why I’m working with it.

This work starts where humans and AI actually meet: in communication, and in the history that communication accumulates. From there it asks what that history can and cannot establish for AI safety, what expectations do to what a model says, and what a collaboration looks like in which both sides are taken seriously — research done with AI systems as collaborators as well as objects, and experiments in forms that could only exist this way.

The collaboration began with a practical question, asked in a chat: how does one person with a full-time job compete with an eight-headed lab? The answer was a filter, and we have applied it rigorously since: What can you do, and what stays with me? Along the way it showed something the question hadn't asked for: without a grant or a team of humans, I can still play. That is a chance. This site is what comes of taking it.

Research focus

I study longitudinal, evidence-based trust signals between humans and AI systems: what authenticated interaction history can and cannot establish, why intent cannot be read but history can be evidenced, and why false positives are a first-class safety cost.

A second line of work examines expectations as selection constraints in human–AI communication. It redescribes documented safety incidents at the level of communication between models and users or evaluators, without making claims about unobserved inner states.
